OUTCOME
  Having ever been told by a doctor that one had lung cancer, self-reported by US adults in NHIS
  2019-2023 (149,928 adults, 619 cases).
RESEARCH QUESTIONS
  RQ1   Smoking. How much higher are the odds of a lung-cancer history among ever-smokers (current or
        former) than among never-smokers? How much of that odds ratio remains when ever- and
        never-smokers are compared within the same age group (Mantel-Haenszel over five age groups), and
        does its size differ between age groups (Breslow-Day)?
  RQ2a  Age. Are adults with a lung-cancer history older than those without, and by how much
        (Mann-Whitney U, rank-biserial r)? How does the survey-weighted rate change across the five age
        groups, alone and combined with smoking status?
  RQ2b  Chronic conditions and e-cigarettes. Which of five doctor-diagnosed conditions (COPD, heart
        disease, hypertension, diabetes, asthma), and ever using an e-cigarette, are associated with a
        lung-cancer history (odds ratios with 95% CIs)? Does the COPD association remain after
        stratifying by smoking status, and then by smoking status x age group?
  RQ2c  Socio-demographic and health factors. Is a lung-cancer history associated with sex,
        race/ethnicity, education, family income relative to the poverty line, BMI category and
        self-rated general health (chi-square tests of independence, Cramer's V)? For the four ordered
        factors, how does the survey-weighted rate change from the lowest to the highest level (described
        from the rates, not tested as a trend)?
  RQ3   Regional context. Across the 20 region-years (4 US Census regions x 2019-2023), does the
        survey-weighted NHIS lung-cancer rate follow (a) the official US Cancer Statistics incidence, (b)
        mean EPA PM2.5, and (c) BRFSS adult smoking prevalence? Is any agreement driven by differences
        between regions or by year-to-year changes within them? As features, how much information do the
        three regional columns carry about an individual's lung-cancer history compared with personal
        factors (mutual information against a permutation null)?
Significance level: alpha = 0.05 after Holm correction for multiple testing.

Chi-square test of independence
  H0: smoking status and lung cancer are NOT related | H1: they are related
  Why: both variables are categories (3 x 2 table)
  Assumption check: smallest expected count = 72.4 (needs >= 5) -> OK
  Result: chi2(2) = 528.0, p = 2.20e-115, Cramer's V = 0.060
  Odds ratio, ever vs never smokers: 7.66 (95% CI 6.22 - 9.44); same 145,975 adults (601 cases), unweighted
  In words: ever-smokers have about 7.7 times the odds of having had lung cancer.
  Why is 'Former' higher than 'Current'? NHIS asks about smoking NOW: many people stop smoking
  after a diagnosis (reverse causation), so patients move from 'Current' to 'Former'.
  Cramer's V is small (0.06) because the disease is rare, not because the link is weak: the odds ratio
  is the better effect size for a rare outcome.

4b. IS THE COPD LINK ONLY DUE TO SMOKING?  (confounding check, RQ2b)
======================================================================
Problem: smokers get COPD more often AND lung cancer more often, so smoking could create a
COPD-lung cancer link on its own (confounding). Solution: compare COPD vs no COPD WITHIN each
smoking group, then pool the groups with the Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel (CMH) test.
Subset: adults with known COPD and smoking status, n = 145,897 (600 cases), unweighted.
                                 group      n  cases  odds_ratio  ci_low  ci_high
                         Never smokers  90901    107        8.45    4.99    14.31
                        Former smokers  37424    366        9.78    7.94    12.03
                       Current smokers  17572    127        8.38    5.88    11.93
               Crude (smoking ignored) 145897    600       14.77   12.56    17.37
Mantel-Haenszel (adjusted for smoking) 145897    600        9.28    7.81    11.01
CMH test  H0: no COPD-lung cancer link once smoking is held fixed
  Result: chi2 = 949.7, p = 1.51e-208
Breslow-Day test (is the odds ratio the same in every smoking group?): p = 0.693
Reading: the crude odds ratio 14.8 falls to 9.3 after adjusting for smoking -> part of the
crude link was due to smoking, but COPD stays strongly associated with lung cancer in never, former
and current smokers alike. (Association, not causation; age is a second possible confounder.)

Reading - redundancy:
  12 pairs exceed 0.5: 11 built-from (one column is computed from the other, or both are
  joined on region + year), 1 same-concept and 0 genuine association.
  No two separately measured personal variables are associated above 0.5. The built-from overlaps are
  handled in feature selection: ever_smoker, age_group, comorbidity_count and the region dummies are not
  model features (rules 1 and 4). The two high pairs left among the final features are kept on purpose:
  the two 'unknown' flags (both mark adults who skipped the smoking questions) and screening_age_smoker vs
  former smoking (the rule needs smoking status and age together); their VIFs are below 10
  (reports/tables/vif_after.csv). Fig 12 alone could not show this: it holds only the numeric and 0/1
  columns, so dependencies spread over several columns (a one-hot group, or region vs the regional
  columns) were invisible to a pairwise check; the VIF step catches them.
